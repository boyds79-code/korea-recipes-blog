Korean fried chicken isn't just fried chicken—it's an engineering feat of crispiness that stays crunchy even under a thick, glossy coat of sweet-spicy sauce. The secret is the double-fry method (더블 프라이), which drives out moisture and creates an incredibly light, shattery crust. Yangnyeom chicken, the sauced version, balances gochujang heat with sweetness and tang in a way that's both addictive and surprisingly balanced.

## Prepare and Season the Chicken

Pat your chicken pieces completely dry with paper towels—this is non-negotiable for crispy skin. Any surface moisture will steam the chicken instead of frying it. Season the chicken all over with salt, pepper, and rice wine if using. Let it sit at room temperature for 15-20 minutes while you prepare everything else. This brief rest helps the seasoning penetrate and takes the chill off if your chicken was refrigerated, which helps it cook more evenly.

## Mix the Coating and Heat the Oil

Whisk together the potato starch and flour in a wide, shallow bowl. Potato starch (or cornstarch if you can't find it) is crucial here—it fries up much lighter and crispier than wheat flour alone. The combination gives you structure from the flour and extreme crunch from the starch. 

Pour your frying oil into a heavy pot or deep skillet until it's at least 2 inches deep. Heat it over medium-high to 160-165°C (320-330°F) for the first fry. If you don't have a thermometer, drop a pinch of the starch mixture into the oil—it should sink briefly, then float up gently with small bubbles. If it sizzles violently right away, your oil is too hot for the first fry.

## First Fry: Low and Slow

Working in batches to avoid crowding, dredge each chicken piece in the starch-flour mixture, shaking off excess, and carefully lower into the oil. Fry for 6-8 minutes (drumettes and wings) or 8-10 minutes (boneless thigh pieces), turning once or twice. The goal here isn't browning—you want the chicken just cooked through and very pale golden. The crust should look almost blonde, not deep brown. Remove to a wire rack set over a baking sheet. Let the chicken rest for at least 5 minutes, up to 10. This rest is when the magic happens: steam escapes, the crust sets, and the interior stays juicy.

## Make the Yangnyeom Sauce

While the chicken rests, make your sauce. In a small saucepan over medium-low heat, combine gochujang, gochugaru, ketchup, honey, soy sauce, vinegar, garlic, ginger, water, and sesame oil. Stir constantly and let it simmer gently for 3-4 minutes until it thickens slightly and the raw garlic smell mellows into something sweet and fragrant. Taste it—the sauce should be punchy, a little sweet, tangy, and as spicy as you like. 

**Adjusting the heat level:** If you want it milder, cut the gochugaru in half or omit it entirely and just use gochujang. For more heat, add extra gochugaru or a pinch of cayenne. The ketchup and honey aren't just for sweetness—they help the sauce cling to the chicken and balance the fermented funk of gochujang. Turn off the heat and keep the sauce warm.

## Second Fry: High Heat for Maximum Crunch

Increase your oil temperature to 180-185°C (350-365°F). This second fry is short and fierce. Return the chicken pieces to the oil in batches and fry for 3-4 minutes until deep golden brown and audibly crackling. You'll hear the difference—the crust should sound crispy and dry, not wet or hissy. Remove to the wire rack and let drain for about 30 seconds.

## Toss in Sauce and Serve

If you want sauced yangnyeom chicken, immediately transfer the hot chicken to a large mixing bowl. Pour about two-thirds of the warm sauce over the top and toss gently but thoroughly with tongs or a large spoon until every piece is evenly coated. The heat from the chicken will help the sauce adhere and glaze beautifully. Add more sauce if you like it heavily coated, but don't drown it—you want to taste the crunch, not just sauce. 

Transfer to a serving plate and garnish with sesame seeds and scallions. For a half-and-half platter (very popular in Korea), toss only half the chicken in sauce and leave the rest plain for contrast.

## Serving and Substitutions

Serve immediately with pickled radish (chicken-mu) if you can find it—the sharp, cool crunch is the traditional counterpoint to rich, spicy chicken. Cold beer or a crisp lager is the classic pairing. 

**Substitutions:** If you can't find gochujang or gochugaru, you can make a simplified sauce with sriracha, ketchup, honey, soy sauce, and garlic, though it won't have the same fermented depth. Cornstarch works perfectly in place of potato starch. Maple syrup or agave can replace honey. For the crispiest results, always do the double fry—skipping the second fry will leave you with soggy, sauce-logged chicken within minutes.
